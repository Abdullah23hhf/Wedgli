Under-Bed Storage Guide

The space under your bed is prime real estate.

The empty space underneath your bed is arguably the most valuable hidden real estate in a small apartment. For renters, using this zone well means storing bulk items — off-season wardrobes, extra bedding, shoes, suitcases — without drilling a single hole or breaking a lease agreement.

By Abdullah · Published September 17, 2026 · Updated October 24, 2026

Choosing the wrong under-bed container can lead to scratched floors, dusty clothes, or bins that jam against the bed frame. This guide breaks down the best under-bed storage solutions for compact urban living.

Choosing the right container material

Before buying storage containers, match the container type to what you plan to store and the flooring you have.

Rolling wooden low-profile drawers

Ideal for easy, daily access. Look for heavy-duty wooden drawers with smooth caster wheels — they slide across carpet, hardwood, and tile without scratching, acting like built-in drawers under a platform bed.

Breathable fabric zippered bags

Perfect for bulk clothing, duvets, and sweaters. Unlike rigid plastic bins that trap moisture and cause mildew, canvas or heavy non-woven bags with clear vinyl tops let fabrics breathe while shielding contents from dust.

Heavy-duty clear plastic bins with latch lids

Best for items prone to moisture or pests, like extra shoes, gym gear, or spare linens. Transparent lids let you see what's inside without dragging the whole bin into the light.

Measure the gap the way a container sees it

Most people measure from the floor to the bottom of the mattress and stop there. That number is wrong more often than it is right.

What limits a container is the lowest point it has to pass. Get down on the floor with a tape and check:

  • Height from floor to the lowest part of the frame, usually the side rails or the slats, not the mattress.
  • The center support leg or beam on queen and king frames, which splits the space in two and blocks long boxes.
  • Leg position. Corner legs can pinch the opening to less than the full width of the bed.
  • Depth, from the edge of the bed to the wall or headboard. Boxes you cannot reach are boxes you will forget.

Standard bed frames typically leave about 6 to 8 inches underneath, and platform beds often leave less, sometimes only a few inches. A box that is 6 inches tall will not fit under a 6-inch gap once you add the lid and the handles. Shop for a container at least half an inch shorter than your tightest measurement.

Rolling bins, lidded boxes and bags

These three behave very differently, so pick by how often you reach in.

Rolling bins are the best choice for anything you touch weekly, like shoes, workout gear or spare bedding. You pull, grab, push. Wheels on carpet can be sluggish, and cheap casters tend to jam on hardwood grit, so sweep before you roll.

Lidded boxes are for the long haul: out-of-season clothes, keepsakes, extra linens. A tight lid keeps dust out. The downside is friction. If getting to the box takes three steps, it will become a place where things go to be forgotten.

Zippered storage bags, the soft, flat kind, are the cheapest way to use a very low gap. They suit comforters and sweaters. They do nothing for hard or heavy items, and they sag at the corners if overfilled.

Rough cost tiers in the US, with prices varying by store: soft bags often run from a few dollars to about $15, basic plastic boxes with lids are commonly in the $10 to $25 range, and rolling drawers with solid wheels can run $25 to $60 or more. Paying more for rolling drawers usually buys sturdier wheels and a frame that does not warp, which matters when the drawer is loaded with shoes.

Unlocking extra height

The biggest mistake renters make is buying storage before measuring their bed clearance. Standard frames often sit only 6–8 inches off the floor, which limits your options fast.

Heavy-duty bed risers

If your frame is too low, safely elevate it with heavy-duty plastic or solid wood bed risers. This instantly lifts the bed 3–8 inches, turning a cramped 6-inch gap into a cavity that can hold deep storage boxes or rolling drawers.

Platform bed alternatives

If you're shopping for a new frame, look for models with built-in hydraulic lift mechanisms or integrated trundle drawers, designed specifically for urban storage efficiency.

How much lift is actually safe

Risers lift the bed a few inches and open up space, but they are also the one piece of this project where a bad choice can hurt someone. Heights vary by product, though many sit in the range of 3 to 8 inches.

Before buying, read the weight rating for the whole set. It should cover the bed, the mattress and the people on it, with a margin, not just the frame. Check that the riser cups or blocks are wide enough for your bed's feet and that nothing is cracked.

Skip risers if the bed is a loft or a bunk, if the frame is wobbly already, or if the bed sits in a high-traffic spot where it gets bumped. Never stack random objects under legs, such as books or bricks. Taller risers also make it easier for a headboard to slide away from the wall, so check that it stays put.

Check your lease too. Some landlords forbid anything that could dent floors, and felt pads under each riser are cheap protection.

Smart categorization & organization

Under-bed storage quickly becomes a graveyard for forgotten items if it isn't organized systematically.

Categorize by frequency of use

  • Outer edge (immediate access): store items you need monthly or seasonally — workout shoes, current-season jackets, extra towels — right at the edge of the bed.
  • Deep center (long-term storage): push winter duvets, formal wear, or rarely-used luggage all the way back.

Label every single bin

Use a label maker or clip-on tags facing outward. It saves you from pulling out every box just to find a pair of gloves or a spare pillowcase.

Incorporate moisture absorbers

Under-bed zones trap stagnant air. Toss a few small silica gel packets or charcoal odor absorbers into fabric bags and plastic bins to keep textiles smelling fresh.

What should never go under the bed

Some things belong elsewhere, even if the space looks perfect.

  • Food and anything that smells like food. It attracts pests, and you will not notice until a problem has settled in.
  • Anything damp. Wet gym bags, towels not fully dry, a drying pair of boots. Closed boxes under a bed trap moisture.
  • Electronics and batteries. Heat, dust and the risk of damage when you shove boxes in.
  • Anything needing steady temperature. Candles, cosmetics, medications, wine, vinyl records, photographs. The air under a bed can run warmer or cooler than the rest of the room, depending on a nearby radiator, vent or exterior wall.
  • Important papers. Passports, leases and tax documents are better in a fire-resistant box or a drawer you can find fast.

Dust, pests and mildew

Dust gathers fast under a bed because nothing moves the air there. A lid is the simplest defense. Once a month, slide everything out and run a vacuum or damp cloth over the floor.

If the floor is on an exterior wall or ground level in a damp climate, add a few silica gel packets or a moisture absorber and check boxes for any musty smell. A smell means something is damp. Dry it fully, air it out, and consider a different place for that item.

For pests, the main defense is not storing food and not keeping paper boxes on the floor if you have had a problem. Plastic with a snug lid is far harder for insects to get into than cardboard.

Hiding the gap without a heavy bed skirt

A bed skirt works, but it is another thing to wash. Alternatives: a long duvet that hangs to the floor, a strip of fabric stapled or tied around the frame (check your lease before using hardware), or simply matching boxes that look neat when visible. A row of identical drawers in a color close to the floor reads as intentional.

A twice-a-year rotation

Under-bed storage works best when it changes with the seasons. Pick two dates, say the first weekend of spring and the first weekend of fall.

  1. Slide out every container and check for damp, pests and dust.
  2. Swap what you no longer need for what you do. Winter coats in, sandals out, or the reverse.
  3. Toss or donate anything you did not miss. If you have not opened a box in a year, you probably do not need it.
  4. Wipe the floor, put the heavier boxes toward the front, and relabel.

A tidy rotation takes about an hour, and it keeps the space from silting up with forgotten things.

Frequently asked questions

How much space do you need under a bed to store things? Even 3 to 4 inches will fit flat bags or shallow trays. Standard frames often leave 6 to 8 inches, which fits most shallow rolling drawers. Measure the lowest point, including rails and the center leg.

Is it safe to use bed risers? They are safe when the weight rating covers the bed, mattress and people, the feet fit securely in the cups, and the bed is stable. Skip them on loft beds, wobbly frames or if your lease forbids them.

Can you store clothes under the bed without them getting musty? Yes, if the clothes are fully dry, the box has a lid, and the room is not damp. Add a moisture absorber if you live in a humid climate and check the contents once a season.

How do you stop dust from collecting under the bed? Use lidded boxes or zippered bags, and vacuum the floor monthly. A bed skirt or low-hanging duvet can also help keep dust out of the open gap.

Quick renter action plan

  • Measure first. Check both the height clearance and the width/depth under your bed frame before buying containers.
  • Prioritize wheels. Wheeled options protect your rental's flooring if you're accessing contents often.
  • Protect the deposit. Avoid modifications that require nailing tracks or permanent fixtures to the floor.
